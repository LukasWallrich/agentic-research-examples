## Answer

**I would not pursue the ITC paper in its proposed form.** The dataset is valuable, but the central result has already been demonstrated: Krajbich et al. found that an apparent faster-SS/slower-LL difference disappeared after accounting for fitted subjective-value differences. They also found that the RT contrast changed with participants’ usual choice: the less common choice tended to take longer. Their ITC analysis had 41 participants, so a careful test across many designs would add evidence, but H1–H2 alone would read as a large replication. [Krajbich et al. (2015)](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms8455)

There is also a **live priority risk**. Pongratz and Schoemann presented a poster titled *Response times in intertemporal choice: Meta-dataset and meta-analysis* in 2024; Pongratz listed a March 2026 talk titled *Response Times in Intertemporal Choice Do Not Support Dual-System Models: Evidence from a Large-Scale Meta-Study*. A talk does not establish what was analysed or published, but the data descriptor’s statement that the dataset had not been used in a *publication* does not mean this question is unclaimed. [University of Mannheim profile](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/smip/team/phd-candidates/cohort-2024/hannah-pongratz/), [ITC Database descriptor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-026-06947-4)

**Outlet judgement:** as written, this is plausible for *Judgment and Decision Making* if executed exceptionally well, but a weak bet for *Cognitive Psychology* or *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*. Those outlets would need a discriminating account of the choice–RT process, not simply an adjusted choice coefficient. *Decision* or *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making* would also require a clearer theoretical advance than “difficulty explains some of the raw difference.” This is an editorial judgement, not a claim about acceptance rates.

### Change the tests

| Proposal | Assessment | Better test |
|---|---|---|
| **H1: one non-zero LL–SS RT difference with a consistent sign** | Poor primary hypothesis. The sign can reverse with the mix of easy LL and easy SS trials and with participants’ baseline preferences. | Estimate the **study-specific raw contrast and its heterogeneity** as a descriptive benchmark. Test whether choice frequency and stimulus design predict its sign. |
| **H2: attenuation after adjusting for fitted \(\lvert\Delta V\rvert\)** | Worth testing, but attenuation is **not an identified “fraction attributable to difficulty.”** The proxy is estimated from the same choices, and different value models imply different difficulty rankings. | Make the primary endpoint a **held-out discrepancy from a prespecified difficulty-only generative model**: does it reproduce the RT-by-choice curves, including reversals across subjects and study procedures? Use cross-fitted participant parameters and compare hyperbolic value difference with choice-predicted entropy and an attribute-based model. |
| **H3: RT improves held-out choice prediction** | Ambiguous. Using the *held-out trial’s completed RT* predicts a choice after that choice was made. That may describe the joint choice–RT distribution, but it is not prospective prediction. | For prospective validity, use **earlier trials’ RTs** to estimate a participant model, then predict a later choice without its RT. Report held-out log loss against a choice-only model. Treat same-trial RT classification as a separate, retrospective result. |

Your simulation is the strongest part **if it makes numerical predictions that can fail**. Simulate choices and RTs independently conditional on latent trial difficulty, using each study’s actual stimuli, participant counts, deadlines, and RT measurement. Fit the full analysis to simulated and observed data; predefine discrepancies such as the raw contrast, its relation to LL frequency, and the residual choice-conditional RT curve. Even rejection would not by itself establish two decision systems: a single evidence-accumulation process can link choice and RT beyond a discounting model’s \(\lvert\Delta V\rvert\). Existing process models already make joint choice–RT predictions. [Dai et al. (2018), cited in the database descriptor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-026-06947-4), [Chen et al. (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-46657-2)

### What could sink it

1. **Prior work, above all.** Krajbich et al. cover the proposed explanatory move, while the database creators have presented a meta-study. RT-assisted preference estimation and held-out prediction also have precedents. The remaining gap is whether a *specific model* survives broad changes in task design. [Krajbich et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms8455), [recent RT preference-model application](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.20403)

2. **The difficulty measure is partly made from the outcome.** Fitting discount rates to all choices and then “explaining” those same choices’ RTs risks overfitting and circular interpretation. Cross-fitting helps, but short tasks and near-exclusive LL or SS choosers may yield unusable individual estimates. Restricting analysis to people who choose both options changes the population being studied.

3. **RT comparability is limited.** The database reports RT in seconds, so a simple milliseconds-versus-seconds error is not the main problem. Response deadlines, presentation, online versus laboratory timing, and studies recording only whole seconds change what an RT means. Analyse log RT within participants and synthesise study-level effects; audit timing and exclusion rules. The database supplies subject and trial exclusion flags, a whole-seconds indicator, and deadlines. [ITC Database descriptor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-026-06947-4)

4. **“100 studies” is not 100 representative replications.** The creators identified 1,709 candidate publications, obtained 112 datasets, and released 100 after permission from the original authors. Participation, data retention, and licence permission select the sample. Several datasets also come from the same publications. Treat generalisation beyond the released studies cautiously. [ITC Database descriptor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-026-06947-4)

### Recommendation among the alternatives

**Choose (c), confidence-weighting harm boundaries, subject to a short data-feasibility check.** The useful question is precise: *under what observable combinations of item-level confidence calibration, between-person calibration, and correlated errors does confidence weighting perform worse than equal weighting?* Compare predeclared aggregation rules on genuinely shared items, with training and evaluation separated by item and participant. The [Confidence Database](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32015487/) supplies a large source of choice and confidence data, but only datasets with multiple people judging the same items can support the empirical test. Check that eligible subset before committing. This also differs substantively from Paper 3’s selective-feedback problem.

It is **not an empty literature**: confidence-weighted voting has been tested, and an algorithm already addresses when *past-performance* weighting is reliable. The contribution must be the boundary for *reported, item-level confidence* under miscalibration and shared errors, validated across eligible tasks. [Confidence-weighted voting study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7960862/), [weighting reliability study](https://doi.org/10.1037/dec0000187)

I would keep **(e) inner-crowd noise structure** as the fallback; repeated-judgement data and the basic aggregation result already exist, so it also needs a sharper model test. [Large natural experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30980050/) **(d) GPS measurement invariance** needs a specified construct, comparable indicators, and groups before its feasibility can be judged. **(b) overprecision** may support a valuable synthesis, but elicitation methods can change the apparent effect dramatically, making “one pooled effect” a poor question. [Soll and Klayman (2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14979805/), [Moore et al. (2015)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597815000989) Reject **(a) as titled**: a sunk-cost meta-analytic review already exists. [Roth et al. (2015)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8)

If you retain ITC, pitch it as a **pre-registered, cross-study failure test of a defined difficulty-only model**, after checking the Pongratz meta-study’s scope. Do not pitch it as discovering that LL and SS choices differ in speed or that difficulty attenuates the difference.
