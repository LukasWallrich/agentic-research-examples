## 2. Evaluation of Specific Domains

### 2.1 Task Boundedness and Scope: Global Adoption vs. Casewise Routing
Objections raised in Round 1 concerning why a decision maker would not route cases dynamically by confidence have been addressed properly:
- The authors treat global adoption as a bounded, constrained decision problem (e.g., selecting an autonomous agent or vendor for a batch of tasks where runtime confidence interrogation is unavailable or costly).
- The manuscript explicitly benchmarks this against the confidence-contingent oracle and evaluates within-stratum pairing. 
- Demanding that a paper on global adoption solve casewise dynamic routing would confuse an objection to the chosen decision task with an error within the task. The scope of global adoption is well motivated, formally bounded, and fully justified.

### 2.2 Numerical vs. Human Empirical Interpretation
The manuscript successfully maintains the distinction between computational consequences and human cognitive mechanisms:
- The estimators ($A-h$, $A-H$, IPW, capped IPW) are treated as candidate decision rules and software-display benchmarks, not confirmed mental operations.
- Monte Carlo intervals and exact convolution checks are properly framed as measures of numerical integration precision and mathematical verification, respectively.

### 2.3 Refutability of the Future Behavioral Extension
The future experimental protocol outlined in Section 4.1 satisfies the criteria for honest scientific refutability:
- It defines a concrete response family ($P(\text{adopt} \mid S) = \lambda/2 + (1-\lambda)\text{logit}^{-1}(\beta S)$) and prevents unfalsifiable post-hoc curve fitting by requiring parameters ($\beta, \lambda$) to be shared across sampling schedules.
- It identifies distinct qualitative orderings on raw histories (e.g., unweighted paired evaluation predicting below-chance adoption in the crossing environment under ratio 4, whereas selection-aware evaluation predicts above-chance adoption).
- It separates display compliance (following provided summary statistics) from unaided learning from raw event sequences, providing diagnostic power to distinguish cognitive updating from heuristic display trust.

### Minor Issues (Actionable)

1. **Clarify Binary Choice Context at First Definition of $D$ in Analytic Framework**  
   In the *Analytic framework*, the text states: *"Let $A$ and $H$ indicate the correctness of the advisor's and human's initial binary responses. Let $D$ indicate disagreement between those responses... On an agreement, $A-H=0$."*  
   In multi-alternative tasks, two incorrect responses can disagree while $A-H=0$. While the text later notes in *Environments and selection* that *"For binary decisions, disagreement is equivalent to exactly one response being correct"*, this assumption should be stated explicitly at the very beginning of the *Analytic framework* (i.e., that the task is a two-alternative forced choice / binary decision, which guarantees that response agreement implies $A=H$ and disagreement implies $A+H=1$).

2. **Formalize Integer-Scaled Increments in the Laurent Polynomial Definition**  
   In *Exact finite-feedback risk*, $u_{mj}$ is introduced as raw score contributions (e.g., $A-h$, which contains fractions such as $0.25$ and $-0.75$), followed by the sentence: *"For rational scenario parameters, multiply by a common positive denominator so that these increments are integers. Define the finite Laurent polynomial $G_m(z)=\sum_j Q_j z^{u_{mj}}$."*  
   To prevent minor formal confusion between the real-valued score increments and the integer polynomial powers, explicitly assign a symbol to the integer-scaled increments (e.g., $\tilde{u}_{mj} = M u_{mj}$, where $M$ is the least common positive denominator) and define $G_m(z) = \sum_j Q_j z^{\tilde{u}_{mj}}$, noting that the decision boundary $\tilde{s} = 0$ and the condition $\tilde{s}\Delta < 0$ are invariant under $M > 0$.

3. **Explicit Definition of Relative Ratio $k$ in Framework Text**  
   In the *Analytic framework*, the verification ratio is introduced as: *"Suppose high-confidence disagreements are verified $k$ times as often as low-confidence disagreements."*  
   To align immediately with the notation in *Environments and selection* ($\pi_H = 1, \pi_L = 1/k$), explicitly state in the framework that $k = \pi_H / \pi_L \ge 1$ represents the relative auditing ratio between high- and low-confidence disagreements.

4. **Case Presentation Order in Raw-History Behavioral Protocol**  
   In Section 4.1 (*A behavioural test that could reject the account*), the raw-history condition displays ordered verified cases to human participants. Because human learners are susceptible to primacy and recency biases, whereas the mathematical estimators treat histories as exchangeable count sets, add a brief explicit note that trial presentation order within generated histories should be pseudorandomized or counterbalanced across participants to prevent order effects from confounding summary statistics.
