## Answer

**I would choose A, but I would make it a preregistered test of *how estimates respond to anchor values*, with the across-item analysis as supporting evidence.** The proposed regression of an item-level anchoring index on anchor extremity is too confounded to distinguish four psychological processes by itself. A is worth pursuing if the preregistration makes model predictions specific enough to fail and gives priority to comparisons of different anchors for the *same question*.

I reviewed papers and dataset documentation only. I did not open or download outcome data. The [2024 correction to the OpAQ paper](https://doi.org/10.5334/jopd.92) adds omitted authors; it does not report a change to the data.

### 1. Can each option fail, and what has already been done?

| Option | A plausible result that would refute its central claim | Closest prior work | Most serious validity threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Anchor extremity** | A fixed model predicts a rising proportional response, but estimates show a reliably flat or falling response for the same items across anchor levels—or the reverse. A pattern outside *all four* preregistered predictive ranges would refute the proposed model set. | [Röseler and Schütz’s OpAQ meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/wf2tn) already examines anchor distance against standardized effects; [Wegener et al. (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1006/jesp.2000.1431) experimentally found that extreme anchors can have *less* influence than moderate ones. [Lieder et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-017-1286-8) and [Turner and Schley (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.07.003) provide formal accounts. | Anchor values, target questions, and response formats were often chosen together. Across-item slope differences can reflect those choices rather than a process response to extremity. |
| **B. Decision noise** | A noise model calibrated independently predicts a task-correlation matrix or held-out choices that excludes the observed pattern, while a model with stable preference differences predicts it materially better. This is plausible, especially if task families differ in ways noise cannot reproduce. | [Frey et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1701381) documented weak correlations between stated and behavioural risk measures and a reliable stated-preference factor. A [September 2026 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12291) already tests stable preferences under noisy measurement and often does not reject them with conventional data. A [July 2026 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05686) shows that standard noisy risk-choice models can themselves generate risk-aversion reversals. Neither is the proposed Basel–Berlin task simulation, but the conceptual space is crowded. | “Noise alone versus stable preferences” is a false split: stable preferences can generate noisy responses. With flexible person- and task-specific noise, the model can absorb nearly any correlation pattern. |
| **C. Noise-only confidence** | The preregistered, zero-bias observer predicts a narrow interval around zero *overall* confidence minus accuracy, and the observed discrepancy lies well outside it. That is possible, though 47 items give limited independent evidence. | [Erev, Wallsten, and Budescu (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.3.519) generated apparent over- and underconfidence from judgment error; [Brenner’s random-support model](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0749-5978(03)00004-9) addresses both; [Moore and Healy (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.115.2.502) distinguish several meanings of overconfidence. | The answer depends heavily on what “zero bias” means and how latent confidence, response noise, and scale boundaries are specified. A well-calibrated observer has zero *expected overall* discrepancy; conditional hard–easy patterns are a different test. |
| **D. Frozen social-influence model** | Parameters fixed using Kao and Jayles predict a distribution of per-question changes in crowd error that misses Becker’s results, including direction, magnitude, or variation across questions. That is a genuine prospective failure. | [Kao et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2018.0130), [Jayles et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2021.0231), and [Becker et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1817195116) already model or analyse how social information changes accuracy; [Becker’s replication archive](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/OE6UMR) includes simulations. The frozen cross-dataset prediction is the distinct contribution. | A failure could come from transferring a revision rule across different social information, networks, and politically charged questions. With one target dataset, it would be hard to separate failed mechanism from failed transport. |

A further caution for **A**: the four slope predictions are currently *interpretations*, not automatic consequences of the named theories. Fixed-size adjustment steps can produce rising AI as anchor gaps grow, but steps proportional to the gap need not. Epley and Gilovich’s plausible-range account was developed chiefly for **self-generated** anchors ([2006 paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01704.x)). And Wegener et al.’s weaker effects for extreme anchors were presented as difficult for a simple selective-accessibility account, so “plausibility-gated selective accessibility” needs its own explicit rule. A recent [four-study test](https://doi.org/10.15626/MP.2024.4137) also failed a formalized insufficient-adjustment prediction. These are reasons to freeze executable models before seeing OpAQ estimates, not to assume that each label uniquely predicts one slope.

### 2. How I would specify A

**(a) Extremity.** Of the three proposed measures, anchor distance from a known true value is the defensible outcome-free one. For a positive quantity with a meaningful ratio scale, preregister the signed values \(\log(a_L/t)\) and \(\log(a_H/t)\), and derive both **gap** \(\log(a_H/a_L)\) and **position relative to truth**. Do not compress them immediately into one “extremity” number: two pairs can have the same gap while one straddles truth and the other sits entirely above it. Truth is a benchmark, **not a measure of any participant’s uncertainty or perceived plausibility**.

This definition excludes or requires a separately specified raw-scale analysis for dates, Celsius temperatures, zero or negative values, and quantities for which ratios lack meaning. Neither the SD of anchored estimates nor the SD of pooled high- and low-anchor estimates is suitable as a primary denominator. Both are outcomes affected by anchoring; pooling can also make the proposed predictor change when the effect changes. Independently collected no-anchor or pretest judgments could estimate uncertainty, but that would be a distinct, preregistered measure available only for eligible items. The [OpAQ documentation](https://openpsychologydata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/jopd.67) distinguishes true values from unanchored comparison estimates, so those must not silently be treated as interchangeable.

**(b) Across-item confounding.** Original authors selected anchors with particular questions, numeric units, intended difficulty, and formats. Anchor gaps may therefore track knowledge, plausible ranges, whether anchors seem informative or random, comparative-question wording, incentives, domain, and response scale. Closed and visual scales impose different constraints. An item’s truth may also sit near a natural lower bound. Random study intercepts do not remove these design correlations. Regression to the mean becomes especially troublesome if an outcome-derived “neutral” estimate or pooled SD enters the extremity measure. Outliers and skew can move means differently across anchor conditions; trimming chosen after viewing them would add researcher discretion. The data paper explicitly documents variation in anchor type, comparative question, scale type, and task type ([OpAQ codebook description](https://openpsychologydata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/jopd.67)).

**(c) Mechanical patterns.** Yes. On a response scale bounded by \(L\) and \(U\), the high-minus-low response difference cannot exceed \(U-L\), while the anchor denominator can keep growing. Falling AI can therefore arise from the scale alone. Division by a small anchor gap creates unstable AI values. Changing from raw to log units also changes the hypothesis: a constant raw-scale weight, \(y=(1-w)b+wa\), gives flat **raw** AI \(=w\), but generally *non-flat* log AI. The direction of that log-scale pattern depends on where the anchor pair lies relative to the baseline. Log transformation, skew, medians, truncation, and asymmetric lower bounds can thus imitate a process signature.

I would preregister the generative scale for each model, run simulated responses through the **same response bounds, transformations, exclusions, and estimator** as the data, and compare predictions on the observed-design scale. A direct model of the high-minus-low estimate difference against the anchor difference is preferable to making noisy ratios the sole analysis. Report bounded versus open scales separately; use a fixed robust-estimation rule and show raw-scale results where raw differences are meaningful. A non-significant slope does not establish the constant-weight account: specify an equivalence region or compare frozen predictive distributions.

**(d) Within-item comparison.** Much stronger, provided the *same question and task format* used multiple assigned anchor values. Holding the target constant removes much of the across-item uncertainty and unit problem. It still requires checking whether anchor levels were assigned comparably and whether other instructions changed with them. With three levels one can assess curvature in the anchor-to-estimate function; a clean comparison of moderate and extreme **high–low pairs** generally needs at least four suitable levels.

Preregister this as a **conditional primary test** without inspecting the file now: define eligible item-by-study cells from design fields alone; require the prespecified minimum number of distinct anchor values, placement around truth, assignment comparability, and independent item/study clusters; fit the specified response curve and model predictions if the gate is met. Set a minimum information threshold using design-only simulation before registration. If the gate fails, report the primary test as infeasible and the across-item analysis as secondary; do not relabel that analysis as an equally strong process test after seeing the data. The OpAQ paper says contributing studies had at least two anchors, **not** that enough items have three or four suitable values ([dataset description](https://openpsychologydata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/jopd.67)).

### 3. What would make A publishable?

The contribution should be a **risky quantitative comparison**, not a new association between distance and effect size. The existing OpAQ meta-analysis and the newer [Schley–Weingarten anchoring meta-analysis](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5893864) already make a broad moderator analysis a thin claim.

Before contact with estimates, I would freeze: model equations and parameter ranges; how any parameters are obtained without using the test outcomes; eligible task and scale strata; the within-item feasibility gate; the estimator and missingness rules; the prediction score and uncertainty procedure; and what magnitude of disagreement counts against each model. Include a simple constant-weight model and explicit **measurement-only null simulations** as benchmarks. Judge success by held-out item or study prediction and by whether each model predicts the observed *shape*, not only an average AI slope. If models make overlapping predictions for the designs OpAQ contains, call the comparison inconclusive.

That package could suit **Judgment and Decision Making** or **Journal of Behavioral Decision Making** even if every proposed process model fails: a carefully diagnosed failure would tell researchers which observable signature their theories do not explain. **Decision** or **Psychonomic Bulletin & Review** would, in my view, require especially clear formal predictions and convincing discrimination between mechanisms. If OpAQ has too little within-item anchor variation, I would still choose A from this shortlist, but describe the resulting paper as a constrained test of **population-level response functions**, not a decisive identification of cognitive process.
